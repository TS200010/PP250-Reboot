## Patent prose — PRIMARY EVIDENCE, separately attributed

### Meanings of instruction fields

Paragraphs (95)–(97) identify the example as a load-data instruction in internal mode and identify IW as the instruction word. Paragraph (96) explains the six fields:

| Printed field | Meaning explicitly supplied by the prose |
| --- | --- |
| ADDRESS | Offset/address information used for the instruction’s data source. |
| C(N) | Capability-register selection defining the block in which that offset is used. |
| M | Data-register selection for address modification. |
| D(N) | Data-register selection for the register receiving the loaded data. |
| FC | Function code defining the load instruction. |
| S | Store-mode flag. |

The prose enumerates these fields starting with ADDRESS and ending with S; the drawing places them left-to-right in the opposite order, as captured above. The prose’s enumeration is not substituted for their drawn spatial order.

### Step S1 — prose account of instruction fetch

Paragraph (99) says the processor uses capability C(7) and the instruction-address register to form the next instruction address. It places the address in MAR; ACC and BCC check permission. The address then travels through BIO, the Figure 3 bus interface, BS & C, BD & T and CBO to the store.

The returning instruction reaches the interface through CBI and BR & T, with the word going over DBIN to IDM and status over SI to BFI. Under CLK control, the word passes over BII to IBL in Figure 2. FC accesses the microprogram unit, which is not shown; address-offset information goes to IREG.

Those named hardware blocks and checks are prose explanations of the S1 box. Figure 6 itself prints only READ IW FROM PROGRAM BLOCK, without any of that circuitry.

### Step S2 — prose account of address formation and internal-mode recognition

Paragraph (101) says the offset in IREG passes through IMUX and BM to ALU A, while the selected capability base from BCB passes through CAPMUX to ALU B. The ALU adds and places the result in MAR. Following the BCC bounds check, BS & C recognises the all-ones module address as internal mode.

Under IM control, IDM loops back the address information. IM also reaches the microprogram unit. The prose gives this transfer sequence, defining := as “becomes”:

```text
BII := BIO
MDIN := BII
ALU := MDIN
ALUCS := ALU
```

Paragraph (106) says the offset information thereby reaches the microprogram unit through arithmetic-unit condition signals ALUCS and selects the required register using the Figure 5 allocation.

Figure 6 does not draw a separate internal-mode decision or loopback arrow. All of this is the prose’s explanation under S2, whose box says only FORM REQUIRED ADDRESS.

### Step S3 — prose account of reading the selected internal register

Paragraphs (108)–(111) say the microprogram unit conditions the required register, bit or selection of bits in the data or capability file to pass through ALU. The transfer sequence printed in the prose is:

```text
MDOR := ALU
BIO := MDOR
BII := BIO
```

This is the prose explanation of READ AT FORMED ADDRESS. The Figure 6 drawing alone does not identify the read source as a particular physical store module or internal register, and does not draw the selected-bit logic.

### Step S4 — prose account of destination loading

Paragraph (113) says data on BII enters MDIN and passes through ALU into the D(N)-selected register in both ADF and BDF. This explains the last box, I/P READ DATA INTO D(N).

The two destination-file copies, MDIN and ALU are not drawn in Figure 6. Their presence and the duplication come from the prose and Figure 2, not from extra symbols hidden within S4.

## Uncertainties and limits

- All six field labels, four step labels and four action texts are reasonably legible. No uncertain letters have been replaced by inferred names.
- I/P is retained literally. Its reading as “input” is consistent with the prose’s description of data entering MDIN, but the drawing supplies no expansion.
- Exact field widths, total instruction length and bit order cannot be recovered from the bar’s proportions alone.
- M is defined by the prose as the address-modifier register selection, but the S2 account does not explicitly describe every operation involving M. No additional modifier operation or datapath has been inserted into the factual drawing.
- The three arrows show the displayed order. They specify neither clock-cycle counts nor timing, pipeline overlap, handshakes or completion tests.
- No return-to-fetch arrow or next-instruction loop is drawn; the capture does not add one from general processor knowledge.
- Internal-mode recognition, protection checks, register selection and BIO-to-BII loopback are in the prose explanation. They are not drawn decision nodes or additional arrows in this figure.
- The existing figure-specific transcription’s filename mentions Figures 4–6 but its contents do not include this flowchart. The complete drawing labels and connections here come from inspection of the PDF.
- These limits concern Figure 6 and its accompanying prose. They do not declare corresponding architectural details unresolved elsewhere in the repository or establish this later patent’s particular instruction format as universal to earlier System 250 configurations.

INTERPRETIVE NOTE

**Explicit figure evidence / DOCUMENTED OBSERVATION:** A six-field instruction layout accompanies a four-stage sequence: fetch the instruction, form the required address, read at that address, and input the read data into D(N). Only forward connections between the four steps are drawn.

**Explicit patent-text evidence / DOCUMENTED OBSERVATION:** The patent uses this load sequence to explain internal-mode access, with address recognition and loopback under S2, selected-register reading under S3, and loading of both destination-file copies under S4.

**Architectural inference:** The figure supplies a common high-level load outline; the prose instantiates it for internal-register access. Its simplicity does not demonstrate that external-store and internal-register operations have identical hardware timing, microinstructions or bus activity.
